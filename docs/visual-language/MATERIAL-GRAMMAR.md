# Academic Concept Lab — Material Grammar

This defines how the drawn layer behaves. It is not a fixed illustration kit.

## Material families

Use as behaviours, not stickers:

- coloured pencil
- graphite
- dry pigment
- repeated pencil passes
- loose contour construction
- hatching and cross-hatching
- scumble, stipple and smudge
- pigment clouds and masses
- rubbed-out traces
- ghost construction
- partial boundaries
- imperfect circles
- wavering underlines
- layered overlaps
- directional marks
- highlight swipes

## Pencil pressure

Pressure can vary from whisper to heavy and may affect width, darkness, density, repeated passes and opacity.

**Non-inference:** darker does not automatically mean more true, more important, stronger evidence or more canonical.

## Hatching

Hatching may be horizontal, vertical, diagonal, curved, irregular or layered. Use it to create mass, volume, overlap, field, direction or local pressure.

Avoid covering whole pages with one repeated hatch language. If every drawing uses the same hatch system, the site becomes mechanical.

## Pigment masses

Prefer repeated transparent marks, visible build-up, broken edges, local gaps, pressure variation and imperfect boundaries.

Avoid solid pastel rectangles, clean gradient panels, generic coloured cards and flat UI fills.

## Overlap

Overlap may express coexistence, relation, competing interpretations, conceptual intersection, layered explanations, ambiguous membership or multiple perspectives.

It should visibly accumulate rather than look like a clean vector Venn diagram.

**Non-inference:** overlap does not automatically mean causation, agreement, hierarchy or equivalence.

## Boundaries

Boundaries may be open, partial, broken, provisional, doubled, rubbed or incomplete. Use them for scope, grouping, category uncertainty, competing organisation or provisional interpretation.

Avoid turning every section into a box.

## Ghost construction

Ghost marks may show earlier construction, alternate organisation, previous position, abandoned relation, underlying geometry or tentative connection.

**Non-inference:** ghost marks do not mean false. They may mean prior, tentative, alternative, under construction or still relevant.

## Erasure

Erasure may communicate revision, uncertainty, rejected simplification, corrected framing or thinking-in-progress. Use lifted pigment, partial erasure, scratch-back, softened gaps or visible residue.

## Directional movement

Directional marks may suggest flow, relation, recurrence, unfolding, attraction or return. Use arcs, S-curves, waves, converging lines, radiating lines, spirals or loose arrows.

Avoid defaulting to clean arrow diagrams.

## Annotation

Annotations should feel earned. Useful functions include warning, question, contradiction, boundary, hidden assumption, overclaim, conceptual shift and unresolved issue.

Strong examples:
- “do not infer a threshold”
- “one event, claimed twice”
- “careful”
- “same note, different role”
- “not the same question”

Avoid filler such as “important”, “remember this” or “key idea”.

## Colour

Use a broad pencil-set palette: cobalt, ultramarine, sky blue, turquoise, teal, emerald, lime, yellow, ochre, orange, vermilion, coral, magenta, violet, lilac, indigo, graphite, charcoal and warm grey.

Do not force every theory to use every colour. Do not map the whole palette to fixed global semantics. Colour behaves primarily as drawing material.

## Material diversity rule

A page should not rely on one repeated technique. Where appropriate, combine clean type, light graphite construction, one or two pigment masses, selective coloured-pencil accents, an imperfect contour, a meaningful annotation, a quieter trace or erasure, and controlled white space.

## Golden rule

> **No semantic mark without intellectual work.**

A mark may expose a changed condition, establish a field, show a relationship, reveal overlap, mark ambiguity, hold a boundary, preserve a prior state, attach a note to a claim, or shape the visual rhythm of an intellectual passage.
