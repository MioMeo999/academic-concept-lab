# Visual grammar

This is a description of the visual language visible in the six reference boards. It is a shared vocabulary, not a second design system. The existing stylesheet defines implementation values, and the art and experience manifests describe registered runtime artwork and page-specific experiences.

The boards give Academic Concept Lab a stable hand and room for different kinds of knowledge. They do not set a fixed palette, a list of required motifs, or a standard page sequence.

## A white editorial canvas

The boards use a bright white field so typography, drawn marks, and empty space can stay distinct. Formal scholarship remains typeset and readable. Drawing appears where it helps the reader notice a relation, movement, uncertainty, contrast, or revision.

Paper fragments, tape, grids, and torn edges appear as possible materials in the boards. They do not require a paper-textured interface or a background made from a full reference board. Use material texture where it does conceptual work. Leave the reading canvas clear when texture would compete with the scholarship.

## Materials and pressure

The boards show graphite, pencil, coloured pencil, crayon, dry pigment, watercolour-like washes, charcoal, ink, and textured marks. Use authored artwork for expressive material when a procedural approximation would look thin or misleading. Keep academic labels and claims in live text.

Pressure changes a mark's weight. A faint line can suggest a provisional trace or a light connection. A darker, denser stroke can hold attention. These are visual possibilities, not fixed meanings. Tell the reader what matters through the surrounding text, and do not let stroke weight imply a measured amount unless the content supports that reading.

Hatching and cross-hatching can create direction, density, or emphasis. Scumbling and stippling can produce broken or granular tone. Smudging can soften a boundary. Layering can show overlap or accumulated attention. Erasure, lifted pigment, and scratch-back can leave a trace of revision. Use these techniques when their material behaviour helps explain the idea. A page need not use all of them.

## Pigment relationships

The boards include cobalt and sky blues, teal and greens, yellow and ochre, orange, vermilion, coral, magenta, violet, warm greys, graphite, and charcoal. This is a broad range, not a required palette.

Let pigments relate to each other within the knowledge being taught. A page may use contrast to distinguish two readings, related hues to keep a family together, or several colours to show coexisting elements. Keep colour choices coherent with the evidence and with the page's purpose. Do not assign universal academic meanings to colours, and do not limit all future pages to three accents.

Colour never carries meaning by itself. Pair it with labels, position, shape, line, pattern, or explanatory text. The existing record kind colours and runtime styles remain governed by the application. This document does not define new CSS tokens.

## Marks as actions

The boards include circles, partial circles, brackets, underlines, highlights, and arrows. They also show dashed or dotted paths, loops, spirals, overlaps, radial fields, clusters, open shapes, and competing strokes. Treat these as actions within a drawing.

A circle may gather attention around a concept or show a provisional group. An open boundary may leave a relation unfinished. A bracket may group items. An arrow may show direction or sequence. An overlapping mark may help a reader compare shared territory. A question mark may locate an open question. Each use needs a local explanation when its meaning is not clear from the academic text.

These associations are not a rigid symbol dictionary. A circle does not always mean a key idea, a dashed line does not always mean uncertainty, and an arrow does not prove causation. Add no mark only because a reference board contains it. Use an arrow for a causal relation only when the evidence and wording support a causal claim. Mark proposed or unresolved relations in words as well as in the drawing.

Repeated, imperfect lines can make construction and revision visible. A residue can show that an earlier reading remains present when the knowledge concerns memory, history, or reinterpretation. Without such a reason, residual marks are visual texture and should not be mistaken for evidence of a process.

## Typography and marginalia

The boards pair large editorial serif forms with readable academic text, compact labels, and handwritten notes. Use type scale and line length to establish hierarchy. Keep scholarly definitions, claims, measures, limitations, citations, and provenance in live text.

Handwritten notes can sound like a human thinking at the margin. They may pose a question, point to a distinction, or give a short editorial reading. They do not replace canonical wording or a source citation. Make the status of a note clear when a reader could mistake an editorial interpretation for an author's claim.

## Composition, scale, and rhythm

The boards range from a broad identity field to focused examples, maps, annotated page fragments, and quiet scholarship. They use scale, grouping, overlap, alignment, contrast, and empty space to direct attention.

Let the central intellectual problem determine what the reader sees first and how much of the argument is visible at once. Whitespace can separate unlike ideas, slow the reading, or give a difficult distinction room. Dense marks can carry a complex comparison when labels remain legible. Alternate dense and quiet passages to set a reading pace. Do not fill open areas merely to make the page look finished.

An opening scene, a map, a layered page, or a quiet typographic passage is an option, not a template. One page may need sustained visual continuity. Another may need a short evidence trail and no major artwork. The content determines the geometry.

On mobile, recompose the idea for a narrow reading field. Preserve the important relations, scale hierarchy, and live labels. Do not shrink a dense board until its words and marks can no longer be read.

## Keep visual explanation inside its evidence boundary

A drawing can help explain an argument, but its presence does not make that argument true. A diagram's position, line weight, direction, and overlap can imply order, size, cause, or certainty. State whether those properties carry information.

Treat teaching examples and mock page content in these boards as visual references. Do not reuse their text as findings, citations, constructs, or claims. Keep source claims, reported findings, paraphrases, teaching examples, and editorial readings distinct. Use the existing provenance model for record content.

Give every explanatory image a useful text alternative or adjacent explanation. Preserve keyboard and touch access for controls. Keep information available without colour, hover, or motion. Follow the application's reduced-motion and responsive behaviour.
