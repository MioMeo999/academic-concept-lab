# Interaction intents

This document records what a reader action is for. It does not prescribe a
component, gesture, layout, motion, or visual form.

## Shared interaction contract

Every interaction should make four things explicit:

1. Action: what the reader can choose, play, reveal, rate, or compare.
2. Variable: what changes between conditions.
3. Invariant: what is held constant.
4. Epistemic boundary: what the action helps the reader notice and what it
   cannot establish about the reader.

All interactive learning must have a linear text or static fallback. Controls
need keyboard equivalence, visible state, touch use, accessible names, and no
essential information hidden only on hover.

## Gestalt Principles in Music

| Intent | Action | Variable | Invariant | Learner should notice | Non-inference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Candidate boundary | Choose and optionally play the two opening presets. | Position of one larger temporal gap. | Note sequence, register, timbre, gain, and overall construction. | The same events can support different groupings when temporal spacing changes. | No universal boundary, perceptual score, musical-ability judgement, or historical replication. |
| Cue competition | Choose time-favoured, register-favoured, or conflict condition. | Temporal gap, register change, or both. | The documented note sequence, tone duration, timbre, gain, and control conditions. | Grouping cues can reinforce, compete, or remain ambiguous. | No required winner and no claim that every listener segments identically. |
| Whole–part relation | Read the paired cases. | Surrounding organisation. | The central event. | A part’s perceptual role depends on the whole in which it is organised. | No mystical whole, fixed law, or claim that the example is a study. |
| Continuation / closure | Inspect alternative descriptions. | The proposed trajectory or completion relation. | The concept being discussed. | Organisation and musical expectation are related but distinct questions. | No equivalence among continuation, cadence, tonic, expectancy fulfilment, or emotion. |

## Tonal Hierarchy

| Intent | Action | Variable | Invariant | Learner should notice | Non-inference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First probe encounter | Choose among the opening probes and listen before revealing the role. | Probe pitch. | The synthetic C-major context and documented audio conditions. | “Home” is a contextual fit metaphor, not a liking measure. | No musical-ability or universal-rating inference. |
| Probe-tone method reconstruction | Choose a probe, listen, optionally give a 1–7 fit rating, and compare the local pattern. | Probe pitch and learner’s optional local rating. | The chosen context, with the record’s stated audio controls. | Context → probe → fit judgment → profile is a measurement logic, not a direct readout of a construct. | Local ratings are learner-generated teaching data, not a published profile, diagnostic score, or replication. |
| Same note / different home | Choose one of four contexts. | Preceding tonal context. | Physical C4 probe, register, timbre, loudness, context length, delay, and probe duration. | Tonal role is relational; the same pitch can be tonic, chord member, or chromatic outsider in different contexts. | No claim that all listeners produce identical judgements. |
| Key neighbourhood | Select progressive levels of key relations. | Representational level: local neighbourhood, fifth relations, major/minor links, advanced toroidal space. | The record’s explanation of psychological similarity. | Key relations are neighbourhoods in a psychological representation, not physical distance. | No literal neural torus or complete key-finding algorithm. |
| Profile versus process | Read the explicit contrast. | Description of ratings versus account of recognition over time. | The distinction itself. | A profile can describe an organisation without specifying the process that builds or revises it. | No claim that the framework is a complete causal theory. |

## Predictive Processing in Music

| Intent | Action | Variable | Invariant | Learner should notice | Non-inference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Message passing | Read the simplified higher-level / lower-level motif. | Direction and function of prediction versus mismatch information. | The canonical distinction as stated in the record. | Direction can be meaningful without supplying a complete cortical or musical wiring map. | No anatomical claim or fixed style → phrase → note hierarchy. |
| Precision comparison | Choose regular or open context. | Width of the constructed prediction envelope (`σ = 35 ms` vs `90 ms`). | Same target, pitch, duration, timbre, gain, and `+120 ms` displacement. | The same deviation can have different model-relative significance under different uncertainty assumptions. | No neural response magnitude, brain distribution, or conscious confidence measurement. |
| Omission | Toggle expected event present / omitted. | Whether a sensory event arrives at an expected onset. | Four preceding beats and the expected position. | Silence can be a mismatch when a model predicts an event. | No diagnosis of the reader’s neural activity and no claim that the construction reproduces the omission study. |
| Model boundaries | Read the distinctions among predictive processing, predictive coding, PCM, IDyOM, precision, and attention. | Construct or explanatory level. | The named definitions and qualifications. | Related predictive vocabularies are not synonyms. | No claim that MMN/MMNm/ERAN/P3 or omission signals are prediction error by definition. |
| Evidence reading | Inspect the Vuust et al. and Ishida et al. X-rays. | Study design, measure, result, and limit. | The evidence-to-claim boundary. | Compatible evidence does not uniquely identify a full predictive-processing computation. | No full architecture proven, no causal training claim, no pure prediction-error signal inferred. |

## Huron’s ITPRA Theory of Expectation

| Intent | Action | Variable | Invariant | Learner should notice | Non-inference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Waiting / timing | Choose on-time or delayed outcome and optionally play it. | Outcome timing. | Preceding context, outcome identity, pitch, harmony, timbre, gain, and tone duration. | Preparation and expectation-related response can be discussed before outcome onset. | No physiological latency or measured tension. |
| Outcome hinge | Read the response windows. | Temporal relation to outcome onset. | The functional definitions of Imagination, Tension, Prediction, Reaction, and Appraisal. | Prediction and Reaction begin in parallel after onset; Appraisal is slower and contextual. | No five-stage algorithm or five independently verified physiological modules. |
| One event / three questions | Choose Prediction, Reaction, or Appraisal lens for the same fixed surprise event. | Interpretive question. | Audio event and preceding context. | One outcome can support different functional questions. | No measurement of reinforcement, reaction, appraisal, or emotional state. |
| Contrastive valence | Read the possible pathway. | Relationship between initial predictive/reactive valence and later appraisal. | The non-deterministic qualification. | Surprise can be evaluated differently at different times. | No universal pleasure mechanism; later surprise findings are not direct ITPRA validation. |
| Expectation sources | Compare schematic, veridical, dynamic, and conscious sources. | Source of expectation. | The four source categories and their concurrent status. | Sources are parallel inputs, not four ITPRA stages. | No claim that one source dominates every listener or event. |
| Evidence reading | Inspect theory, expectancy-violation, statistical-learning, and surprise/pleasure X-rays. | Design and evidential status. | Source-specific tested/found/not-tested fields. | Convergent expectancy evidence is not proof of five isolated ITPRA systems. | No complete emotion theory, neural model, or universal musical-pleasure account. |

## Cross-record interaction invariants

- Selection changes the explanatory condition, not the record’s truth.
- A control state must be narratable in text.
- Any learner-generated rating or response remains learner-generated data.
- Audio controls must disclose pitch/timing/context details when those details
  matter to the claim.
- Interactions expose distinctions; they do not collect hidden research data.
- A redesigned interaction may change the action while preserving the same
  variable, invariant, epistemic purpose, and boundary.
