# Theory manifest: Tonal Hierarchy

- Record kind: `theory`

  complete causal theory of key detection.

## Core problem

Which pitch feels like a tonal home in a given context, and how does the role of
the same physical pitch change when the surrounding tonal context changes?
What do probe-tone judgments and psychological representations measure, and what
do they leave unexplained about key finding over time?

## Central claim

Tonal function and stability are relational properties of pitch-in-context.
Probe-tone and related psychological judgments reveal structured differences
among tonic, tonic-triad, other diatonic, and nondiatonic relations while also
retaining pitch-height and chroma/octave-equivalence dimensions.

## Primary concepts

- tonal context, tonic, diatonic collection, nondiatonic/chromatic tone
- tonic-triad membership and relative fit/stability
- probe-tone task and completion/fit judgment
- empirical probe-tone profile versus psychological interpretation
- pitch height and chroma/octave equivalence
- major/minor profiles and key neighbourhoods
- circle-of-fifths, relative, and parallel relations
- multidimensional/toroidal representation as psychological similarity space
- dynamic tonal organisation as successive evidence accumulates
- musical exposure, distributional experience, development, and culture
- profile versus the process of recognising, maintaining, or revising a key

## Mechanism and relationships

The record’s measurement logic is:

`tonal context → probe tone → fit/completion judgment → repeat across pitch
classes → empirical profile → inference about tonal organisation`

This is a measurement-to-interpretation chain, not a complete cognitive
process. A more abstract relation is:

`pitch × current context → tonal role/relative stability`

As successive chords arrive, earlier evidence remains relevant while new
evidence changes the balance among competing tonal interpretations. A tonal
profile can describe a pattern without specifying the complete time-dependent
key-finding process.

## Important distinctions

- Fit/completion rating is not liking, pleasure, emotional valence, or musical
  ability.
- A profile is not a property of a physical pitch independent of context.
- Tonal stability is not identical to next-event probability or Meyerian
  expectancy.
- Tonal hierarchy is not equal to pitch-class frequency counts.
- The Krumhansl torus is a psychological representation, not a literal neural
  map.
- Western major/minor organisation is not universal tonal cognition.
- Developmental differentiation is not an immutable innate staircase.
- A profile is not a complete key-finding process.

## Temporal, hierarchical, and representational structure

The record moves from a local context/probe relation to qualitative pitch
hierarchy, then to key relations and dynamic change. The hierarchy is not a
single vertical scale: pitch height, chroma, tonal function, key neighbourhood,
learning history, and cultural system all contribute distinct relations.

## Evidence structure

| Evidence | What it tests or supports | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Krumhansl & Shepard (1979) | Context-dependent completion judgments reflecting pitch height, octave equivalence, and tonal-function hierarchy. | Not liking, universal next-note probability, or complete key finding. |
| Krumhansl (1979) | Psychological pitch organisation beyond physical frequency distance, including height, chroma, triad, and diatonic relations. | Not literal neural geometry or one key-detection process. |
| Krumhansl & Kessler (1982) | Key relations and changing tonal organisation represented through profile similarity and a four-dimensional toroidal topology. | Not a literal brain map or fixed profile throughout a piece. |
| Krumhansl & Keil (1982) | Developmental differentiation among scale, tonic-triad, and other tones in short-sequence judgments. | Not an innate universal stage model or an adult probe-tone replication. |
| Castellano, Bharucha & Krumhansl (1984) | Immediate context and culturally specific rag/thāṭ relations in North Indian tonal judgments. | Not universal Western hierarchy or a complete culture model. |
| Butler (1989) | The profile/process and task-interpretation problem. | Does not reject context-dependent tonal organisation. |
| Temperley & Marvin (2008) | Pitch-class distribution can support above-chance key identification but is insufficient alone. | Tonal hierarchy is not identical to frequency counts. |
| Constructed lab | The logic of context, probe, optional fit rating, qualitative profile, and same-note comparison. | Not an exact replication or diagnostic assessment. |

## Scope and stopping points

Explains: context-dependent pitch fit, relative tonal stability, tonic
centrality, tonal-function differentiation, psychological pitch relations,
major/minor profiles, key similarity, and dynamic changes in tonal organisation.

Stops at: all melody perception, all harmonic processing, all expectation,
emotion/preference, universal tonality, complete key finding, and literal neural
key-space geometry.

## Interaction intent

The probe lab lets the reader choose a probe and optionally record a local fit
rating. The same-note comparison holds the physical C4 probe constant and
changes only the preceding context. The key-space view changes representational
level, not the underlying claim. These actions teach contextual dependence and
measurement logic; they do not measure the learner’s ability or establish a
published profile.
